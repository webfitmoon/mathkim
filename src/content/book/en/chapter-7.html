Opening Story: “If We Keep Working on School Exams, When Do We Ever Prepare for the CSAT?”

This was the worry Hyunwoo’s mother raised most often in our consultations. “Sir, if we keep on top of school exams, there’s no time even to touch CSAT study. Won’t he collapse on the CSAT in grade 12?” Many parents treat school exams and the CSAT as two separate tests. Trying to prepare each one independently exhausts child and parent alike.

School exams and the CSAT differ in format and preparation. But the concepts you learn can serve both. Starting from the 2028 CSAT syllabus, we will look at what to prepare together and what needs separate practice.

The 2028 CSAT: Start from the Common Syllabus

The mathematics examination changes from the 2028 CSAT onwards. The old system, in which candidates chose one elective from among advanced calculus, probability and statistics, and geometry, is abolished. Every candidate is examined on the same common syllabus: Algebra, Calculus I, and Probability and Statistics.

The significance of this change is greater than it first appears.

  • The courses you study for grade-11 school exams
    — Algebra, Calculus I, and Probability and Statistics — are the CSAT syllabus itself.
    You can use this shared syllabus for both school study and CSAT preparation. The year in which each course is taken depends on the school curriculum.

  • Calculus II and Geometry in the 2022 revised curriculum are not included in the material directly examined by the 2028 CSAT. The dilemma of choosing an elective has disappeared too.

There is no need to treat school study as unrelated to the CSAT. Learn the concepts thoroughly at school, then practise the CSAT’s question format and time management separately with past papers and official sample questions.

How Grade-10 and Grade-11 School Exams Lead into the Grade-12 CSAT

Connecting school study with CSAT preparation gives the following three-year plan. Adjust when you study each course to your school’s curriculum.

  • Grade 10, Common Mathematics 1 and 2: the root of everything. The roots of equations, functions, and inequalities grow directly into grade-11 Algebra and Calculus I. If Common Mathematics is weak, the grade-11 courses shake from end to end. Every year, I see students who dismissed grade-10 Common Mathematics as “easy” collapse in grade-11 Calculus I. It is not because they cannot differentiate. The functions and equations underneath are weak. Alongside differentiation practice, check the functions and equations on which it depends.

  • Grade 11, Algebra, Calculus I, and Probability and Statistics: the CSAT syllabus itself. Concepts learned deeply here become foundations for later CSAT problems. Practise using them in unfamiliar contexts too.

  • The important point is that earlier material is used again. If a new unit stops you, revisit the earlier concepts its solutions depend on.

In grade 12, leave ample time to consolidate earlier work and apply it under exam conditions. Some schools teach particular courses for the first time in grade 12, so also check what you have not yet covered. The CSAT is not an exam that begins in grade 12. It has already begun with the first midterm of grade 10.

One warning must be made plainly here. Around grade 11, a certain kind of student always appears: “I’m giving up on school exams and going all in on regular admissions through the CSAT.” They call themselves “CSAT fighters.” Which admissions route deserves more weight depends on the student’s record and the target university’s plan. But focusing on the CSAT is no reason to neglect the concepts being taught at school.

Think about it. Algebra, Calculus I, and Probability and Statistics, learned for school exams, are the CSAT syllabus. Abandoning school exams means walking out, under your own power, from the basic training ground for the very material that will appear on the CSAT. Use the weaknesses revealed by school exams in your CSAT study too. Deciding which test deserves more time is a different question from learning the necessary concepts properly.

A Three-Year Roadmap That Does Not Wobble

Built on this structure, here is the focus for each year.

  • Grade 10: the year to plant roots and methods. Treat Common Mathematics 1 and 2 not as courses to finish quickly but as the roots to secure most firmly. At the same time, make the methods from Chapters 1–5 — solving by hand, managing time, weaving a concept net — second nature. Habits formed here last for all three years.

  • Grade 11: the year to deepen the shared syllabus. You study Algebra, Calculus I, and Probability and Statistics. Use school study to learn the concepts in the common CSAT syllabus, and give those exams everything while adding the past-paper analysis from Chapter 4 and viewing them through “CSAT eyes” too.

  • Grade 12: the year to consolidate and sharpen. Check which parts of the syllabus you have not yet studied. Bring the concepts built in grades 10 and 11 up to exam speed and repair weak units. If the previous two years were weak, the year is consumed by repairs; if they were secure, you can leap into advanced work.

One tip for the High School Credit System: this is now an age in which students choose their courses. But there need be no confusion about mathematics. Whatever your intended field, humanities or sciences, Algebra, Calculus I, and Probability and Statistics are the common CSAT syllabus and courses everyone must be sure to take. If you aim for medicine or the very top science programmes, however, Calculus II and Geometry, although not directly examined on the CSAT, may matter as completed courses on the school record or as recommended preparation for the intended major. Whether a university considers them in its own written examinations or interviews must be checked separately in that university’s admissions plan. It is worth taking them selectively, according to where you aim to go.

Parents’ Q&A

One Page for Parents

Q.
The CSAT is changing again. I have no idea how to prepare my child.
A.
This change is actually welcome news for parents. With electives gone, there is no longer a dilemma over what to choose, and the courses learned for school exams are now the CSAT syllabus. Help your child learn the concepts deeply at school, and practise the CSAT’s question format and time management separately.
Q.
My child is in grade 10. Do we really need to think about the CSAT already?
A.
You need not worry about it. It is enough to know that grade-10 mathematics is the root of the CSAT. Not skimming over Common Mathematics now is already the greatest investment in the CSAT three years ahead. There is no need for haste, but this is not a period to treat lightly.
Q.
My child intends to follow the humanities track. Must they still take Calculus I?
A.
Yes. They must. Beginning with the 2028 CSAT, Algebra, Calculus I, and Probability and Statistics form the common syllabus regardless of humanities or sciences. Dropping Calculus I because your child is in the humanities is like leaving one entire pillar of CSAT mathematics empty. Every student must secure all three courses, whatever their intended field.
Q.
My child is already in grade 12. Is it too late to change the method now?
A.
It is not too late. Check the time remaining and any unstudied material, then start repairing the weakest unit with the methods in this book. Using the remaining time with the right method is always better than giving up because it feels late.

In Closing: Three Years Are One Road

See school exams and the CSAT as two mountains and you will be chased for three years. See them as one road and the direction becomes clear. The common syllabus of the 2028 CSAT provides a basis for connecting preparation for both tests. Grade-10 roots, the grade-11 syllabus, grade-12 depth: these are not three disconnected tasks, but one journey along a single line.

A Short Pause

A Slump May Be a Sign That Your Ability Is Growing

When grades stay still or even fall despite hard work, students often collapse into the thought, “Maybe this is as far as I go.” But over many years I have often seen that plateau arrive just before ability leaps to the next level. During the transition from solving by eye to solving by hand, and from memorising problem types to understanding concepts, the familiar old method is breaking down while the new one has not yet settled. For a while, the grades wobble.

Picture a staircase. It is not a diagonal slope but a series of flat stretches alternating with vertical rises. Ability grows the same way. For a time, nothing seems to change; then suddenly you step up a level. Give up on the flat stretch and you never reach the next step.

So when a slump arrives, first check whether the method is wrong. But if you are confident that the method is right, hold on a little longer. Today’s frustration may not be retreat, but the run-up to your next leap.
