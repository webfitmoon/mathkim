Opening Story: Two Students Who Worked Through the Same Past Papers

Some years ago, two students in the same class, Jiho and Taeyun, each worked through the same collection of their school’s past papers three times. They completed roughly the same number of questions and spent roughly the same amount of time. But Jiho focused each time on getting the answer right, while Taeyun asked of every question, “Why was this set?” From the outside, Jiho seemed to work harder: his rounds were faster and his tally of correct answers higher. Taeyun would hold on to one question and puzzle over it for so long that progress looked painfully slow.

Three months later, in the exam, the two reacted completely differently to an unfamiliar question. Jiho panicked: “This wasn’t in the past papers.” Taeyun calmly put pencil to paper: “Ah, they’ve twisted that concept this way.” They had seen the same papers, but Jiho had memorised the questions while Taeyun had read the examiner. From that day, I had Jiho follow Taeyun’s method. At first came the complaint, “If I go this slowly, when will I ever finish the material?” But after solving an unfamiliar question unaided in the second exam, Jiho never returned to the old way.

This chapter is not about how many times to solve past papers. It is about how to take them apart.

The Work Continues After You Mark a Past Paper

Many students use past papers like another problem book. They solve, mark, move on when they are right, and read the worked solution when they are wrong. Used this way, past papers are nothing more than a bundle of questions. Once solved, their useful life is over.

But the real value of a past paper lies not in the question itself, but in the examiner behind it. With school exams in particular, the teachers setting the paper are known, and the concepts they value, the types they return to, and the traps they favour are all preserved in past papers. A past paper is the most honest trailer for what will appear on the next exam, and how. Close it after checking the answer and you have left the cinema without watching the trailer.

Got It Right? Dissect It All the More

Most students revisit only the questions they got wrong. Top students dig even more fiercely into the questions they got right. They ask three things.

① Was It Ability, or Was It a Guess?

A question answered by guessing or by a vague feeling is not “one you got right.” It is one you simply have not got wrong yet. Twist it even slightly next time and you will. Even for a correct answer, check that you can explain, “This is the answer, for these logical reasons.”

② Was There a Faster Route?

If the answer was right but took five minutes, that solution is a “dangerous correct answer” in the exam room. The habit of looking for another route that solves the same question in two minutes attacks at its root the time problem discussed in Chapter 3.

③ Why Did the Examiner Give This Condition? Read the Pattern

This is the heart of it. We extend the “tracing back” of Chapter 2 across an entire set of past papers. Gather three years of one school’s papers and you will see the same tool returning each year in a different face. Suppose one year asks for the sum of the two roots of a quadratic equation, the next for their product, and the next for the distance between them. The three may look different, but all grow from one tool: the relationship between roots and coefficients. A student who reads that pattern walks into this year’s exam already prepared for whatever new face that tool may wear.

We need not look far for an example. The questions we worked through together in Chapter 2 are precisely like this. One asked for the distance between the two roots of x2−4x+3=0 (the roots 1 and 3, a distance of 2). Another asked for the value of k that makes the distance between the two roots of x2+2x+k=0 equal to 4 (k=−3). We solved the first by factorising and the second through the relationship between roots and coefficients. The first can also be approached through the sum and product of the roots, using (α−β)2=(α+β)2−4αβ.

If one year’s paper asks for the sum of two roots, another for their product, and another for their distance, those are not three different questions. They are one tool appearing three times in disguise. The Chapter 2 examples let you practise making that connection. When you study actual past papers, look beyond appearances to the concepts used together.

Classify Your Wrong Answers: There Is More Than One Way to Be Wrong

Simply solving a missed question again is only half a review. What matters is why you got it wrong, and not all wrong answers are the same. Divide them into at least four categories.

  • Concept gap — You did not know it at all. → Return to the concept and learn it again.

  • Calculation error — You knew it, but slipped in the calculation. → Correct it with the habits of writing out the working and checking it from Chapter 2.

  • Missed condition — You failed to see a condition such as “distinct” or “positive.” → Correct it by circling conditions, as in Chapter 1.

  • Ran out of time — You could have solved it, but had no time. → Correct it with the time management from Chapter 3.

Each may be a “wrong answer,” but each needs an entirely different prescription. Try to cure a calculation error with more concept study and you merely waste time. Dismiss a concept gap as “just a mistake” and you will get it wrong again. Classify the reason accurately and half the review is already done.

I once classified a student’s exam paper in precisely these four ways.

  • Could not find the maximum of a quadratic function → did not know the concept of the vertex itself → concept gap

  • Set up the discriminant correctly, then copied a sign the wrong way round → calculation error

  • Missed the condition “two distinct roots” and solved using D≥0 → missed condition

  • Could readily have solved the question but ran out of time before touching it → ran out of time

All four looked identical under the heading “wrong,” yet their prescriptions were completely different: review the concept, build the habit of checking, circle the conditions (Chapter 1), and manage time (Chapter 3). Had we lumped them together as “wrong, so solve it again,” that student would have fallen in exactly the same places in the next exam.

The Right Form for an Error Notebook

An error notebook is not a place to copy out missed questions beautifully. A notebook that takes thirty minutes just to transcribe a question is close to a waste of time. You need exactly two lines: “Why was I wrong? (Which category?)” and “What will I change next time?” An error notebook with those two lines is more powerful than one in which you have copied the question a hundred times.

Place two students’ error notebooks side by side and the difference is stark.

[A Typical Error Notebook] Cut out the missed question and paste it in, then use three coloured pens to copy fifteen lines of the worked solution beautifully. Time: twenty minutes. What remains is the satisfying feeling of having studied.

[An Effective Error Notebook] “Cause: copied a sign the wrong way round (calculation error) / Prescription: compare the signs in the copied expression with the original.” Time: two minutes. What remains is a check to use on the next solution.

A pretty error notebook gives you the feeling of having studied. What changes the mark is usually the ugly notebook with two lines.

Action Check

Three Things to Change Today from This Chapter

  1. Do not “solve and mark” past papers; “solve and analyse” them. Explain why even a correct answer is correct.

  2. Classify each wrong answer into four types: concept, calculation, condition, or time, and write down the prescription that fits.

  3. Do not copy the question into your error notebook. Leave only two lines: “Why was I wrong? + What will I change?”

Parents’ Q&A

One Page for Parents

Q.
My child puts enormous care into an error notebook, but the grades do not rise to match.
A.
Check whether the time is being spent making the notebook pretty. Copying the question exactly and decorating it with coloured pens may look like study, but the actual effect is weak. Ask one thing: “Why did you get this question wrong?” If your child can give the reason unaided, the review is already complete, however plain the notebook looks.
Q.
How many times is enough for working through past papers?
A.
The method matters more than the number. Solve them three times while merely checking answers and nothing remains. Solve them once while asking “why was it set this way?” and the examiner’s pattern remains. When your child starts saying, “So this school likes questions like these,” they are using past papers properly.
Q.
My child tries to move on by saying, “I only got this wrong by mistake.”
A.
For a student, the word “mistake” is the sweetest and most dangerous form of self-comfort. First distinguish a genuine slip in calculation from confusion caused by a weak concept. Say, “If it was a mistake, write exactly one line on why it happened.” If your child cannot write that line, it was not a mere slip. It is a weakness still in hiding.
Q.
Past papers from the school are difficult to obtain. What should we do?
A.
In truth, the best past papers are already in your child’s hands: the last midterm and final papers they sat. Before hunting down somebody else’s papers, have your child dissect their own previous exams. The habits of that school, and of that teacher as an examiner, are already there in full.

In Closing: Past Papers Are a Window onto the Future

Past papers are not dead questions. They are a window illuminating the exam to come. Solve them to get the answers and they become a problem book you use once and throw away. Take them apart to read the examiner and they become a map of what will appear, and how.

We now have the skills for approaching a problem (Chapter 2), commanding the clock (Chapter 3), and reading past papers (Chapter 4): the craft of sitting an exam. Yet every one of those skills stands on a single foundation: concepts. The next chapter goes into the real fork separating top students from everyone else, namely the difference between “knowing” a concept and being able to use it.

A Short Pause

The Focus Created by Five Minutes of Clearing Your Desk

When studying will not start, the problem is often the desk, not your head. Sit down among yesterday’s handouts, a problem book for another course, a phone, and a snack packet, and your mind is scattered before you begin. To focus on one thing, first remove from sight the things that compete for your attention.

I am not proposing a grand clean-up. For exactly five minutes before you study, keep only the book and notebook for the subject you are about to work on, and one pen, on the desk; move everything else out of sight. Do not just put your phone on silent. Put it in another room. If you keep checking it, leave it somewhere you cannot easily pick it up during the study session.

Clearing the desk is really practice in clearing the mind. It is a ritual for telling yourself: “From now on, I will focus on this one thing.” When you are frustrated that your ability is not growing, try clearing the desk once before changing how you study. Surprisingly often, that is where the problem lies.
