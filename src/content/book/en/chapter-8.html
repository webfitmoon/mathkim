Opening Story: The Day Hyunwoo Received Grade 4

In the first chapter of this book, Hyunwoo received Grade 4 on his first high-school midterm. The story I will tell now is, in fact, the story of that evening: the evening he received the shocking report card. Before he could change how he studied, the first thing Hyunwoo had to overcome was not a thick problem book, but the loss of confidence to begin again.

That day, Hyunwoo closed his bedroom door and did not come out for a long time. “Maybe I simply don’t have a head for math.” It was the moment when he was ready to call everything a matter of talent and give up. Dinner went cold on the table. His mother later told me that she had paced outside the closed door several times, then simply walked away because she did not know what to say. “If I pushed him to study more, I thought he might break. If I said it was all right, that felt like a lie….” That was how she had felt that night.

In truth, the most frightening thing that night was not the number Grade 4. It was the label taking shape in a parent’s mind: “What if my child truly cannot do math?” Had Hyunwoo actually given up then, none of the methods in this book would have mattered. Every method of study works only on top of a willingness to try again.

This chapter is therefore the last in the book and yet, in truth, the story that is needed first. It is about how to rebuild a spirit that has collapsed.

A Report Card Is Not a Verdict but a Diagnosis

Do badly on the first exam and it feels as though the world has ended. Under the five-band system, especially, one mistake can appear to move a grade sharply. But a report card is not a verdict declaring, “This is as far as you go.” It is a diagnosis saying, “This is where you are now, and this is the way forward.”

A doctor reads a diagnosis and writes a prescription instead of sinking into despair. Read a report card in the same way. Instead of being crushed by a Grade 4, ask, “Where and why did I go wrong?” using the classification from Chapter 4. At that moment, the report card ceases to be grounds for despair and becomes a blueprint for the next exam. And remember: the first exam of grade 10 is only the first five minutes of a long, three-year match. No match ends because you conceded a goal in the opening five minutes.

Trust the Process, Not “Talent”

At the root of a collapsed spirit lies a misunderstanding called talent. The belief that “you need a good head to do well at math” turns one failure into an apparent verdict on talent. From beginning to end, this book has tried to prove the opposite. Math is method, not talent, and method enters the body through a process.

Trust the process, then, not the result. Think of strength training. Lift a barbell today and you will not see new muscle in the mirror tomorrow morning. Work through math with the right method today and, likewise, it will not show up in tomorrow’s marks. Even before the marks change, check whether you can now explain an idea or complete a solution that stopped you yesterday. One problem written out by hand today, one question’s intent traced back today: even if neither appears immediately as a mark, that time has not been wasted.

Change whom you compare yourself with, too. Not the student beside you, but the person you were yesterday. If you understand one concept more precisely than you did yesterday, that day’s study has succeeded. These small successes accumulated, unnoticed, until they turned Hyunwoo’s Grade 4 into Grade 2. It was not a miracle. It was the change created by time spent trusting the process.

The Parent’s Role: Pacesetter, Not Supervisor

From here, I am speaking to parents. Your role in your child’s mathematical journey is decisive. But it must be the role of a pacesetter, not a supervisor.

Surveillance and pressure alone do little to help a child learn to work independently. Instead, become the pacesetter alongside your child, matching their breathing. The specific method is exactly what I have repeated at the end of each chapter. Ask not “What mark did you get?” but “Will you explain just one of the problems you solved today?” Ask about the process, not the mark.

Suppose your child has just had an exam go badly. “After all the time you’ve spent at the academy, how can these be your marks?” is the worst thing you can say: it turns the result into a weapon of guilt. Ask this instead: “What was different between the problem book you worked through at the academy and the questions on this exam?” Both questions concern the exam. The first closes your child’s mind; the second lets them analyse the cause for themselves.

Above all, do not load your own anxiety onto your child. A child reads their future in a parent’s expression. Only when the parent first believes, “It’s all right; we can change the method,” can the child pick up the pencil again. What brought Hyunwoo back to his desk that evening was not, in truth, a method of study. It was the sentence, “Let’s try again.”

Action Check

What to Hold On To from This Chapter (for Students)

  1. A report card is not a verdict but a diagnosis. Do not collapse under the number; read the “why.”

  2. Do not compare yourself with others. Compare yourself with who you were yesterday.

  3. Trust the process even when the result is invisible. The one problem you solve today is not wasted.

Worksheet

Compare Yourself with Yesterday

The person to compare yourself with is not the student in the next seat, but the person you were yesterday. Comparison with others leaves you helpless; comparison with yesterday gives you the strength to keep moving. Write exactly three lines today.

Sample Entries
· One thing that went better than yesterday: Today I solved, unaided, a type of quadratic inequality I could not solve yesterday.
· Problems I wrote out by hand to the end today: 4
· A word to myself tomorrow: I may be slow, but the direction is right. Keep going.

Today’s record:

One thing that went better than yesterday
Problems I wrote out by hand to the end today
A word to myself tomorrow

Not the number on the report card, but the accumulation of these three lines: that is the real evidence that ability is growing.

Parents’ Q&A

One Page for Parents

Q.
What should I say to a child devastated by a bad first exam?
A.
“It’s all right. This is only the beginning. You do not lack a head for math; you simply haven’t changed the method yet.” Begin by acknowledging how your child feels. When they are ready to talk, listen to what was difficult and look together for something they can change.
Q.
My child wants to give up math altogether.
A.
The helpless belief “Nothing I do will work” may be part of it. First listen to what your child is finding difficult. Instead of presenting a grand goal, create one very small success: help your child solve today one problem they could not solve yesterday.

In Closing: In the End, the Spirit Comes First

In this book we have discussed a great many methods, from how to solve a problem to a three-year roadmap. But every one of them works only on a single foundation: the willingness to try again. Hyunwoo’s true beginning was not any method of study. It was the spirit with which he picked up his pencil again on the evening he had collapsed.

The methods are all here in this book. All that remains is to trust them and take the first step.
