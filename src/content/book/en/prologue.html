University of Cambridge, Mathematics (BA, MA) · College Scholar (awarded for academic distinction).
Yonsei University: first-round admission to Dentistry (2022) and Pharmacy (2024) through the essay-based track.

People hearing my background for the first time usually ask the same question. “With a record like that, shouldn’t you be teaching the top students in their final year, or a medical-school essay class? Why, of all things, grade 10 and 11 school exams?”

The answer is simple. I wanted to prove something.

Among people gathered from every corner of the world, people who really had been “born with a head for math,” what I discovered was, unexpectedly, the exact opposite of what I had expected. The way a person approached a problem, their method and attitude, mattered as much as the ability they started with. I wanted to find out with my own hands whether that single principle would hold just as well in the unfamiliar, unforgiving world of Korean entrance-exam mathematics.

So I, by then a teacher, became a candidate again and sat down in front of the very same paper as that year’s high-school seniors. And against odds of more than a hundred to one, I was admitted in the first round to Yonsei University’s dentistry and pharmacy programmes, one after the other, through the essay-based track.

Not because I am some born genius. I had simply worked out, precisely, the pattern of decoding that Korean math exams demand. Whether it is pure mathematics at Cambridge or the school exam you will sit tomorrow, mathematics in the end is not a talent. It is a craft of decoding, and a craft is won by training.

I know this: at this very moment, countless tenth- and eleventh-graders are sitting at their desks until eleven at night, swallowing the unfairness of it all. They have worked through three problem books, kept an error notebook, sat through every online lecture that is supposed to be the best, and this midterm score is exactly the same as the last one. And the parents who watch them from behind are eaten up with worry.

Are you, right now, blaming yourself for not having been born with a head for math?

A stalled score is not a reason to question your ability or effort first. Before adding more hours, look at how you spend them. Can you solve a question unaided after thinking you understood it? Do you keep getting stuck at the same point?

“In the end, math is all about the head.” “A head for math is something you are born with.” I am convinced these sayings are half a lie. What looked like talent turns out, on inspection, to be a very precisely engineered set of habits.

That is why I focus on school mathematics in grades 10 and 11. Difficult final-year questions depend on concepts and habits learned earlier. Repair a gap revealed by the first grade-10 midterm, and later material becomes easier to take in. This does not mean the contest is over early. It means there are things you can change early.

To the parents who have opened this book, a word as well. I know what it feels like when the hours your child spends at the desk keep growing and the grades do not move. Should we add another private academy? Is our child just not built for math? Please do not rush to that conclusion. Before adding another academy, look with your child at where the current work is getting stuck. At the end of every chapter, and in the final chapter, Chapter 8, this book speaks frankly about the parent’s role too: how to stand firmly beside your child while that method takes hold.

Take the method written in this book and apply it for one semester exactly as this book lays it out rather than in the way you are used to. Replace the habit of following with your eyes with the habit of writing it down by hand. Start tracing the examiner’s intent backwards in the problems you got right as well as in the ones you got wrong.

Drop the wrong method, get onto the right track, and you will be able to see, step by step, what has changed. Check the process first, before you check the score.

— Sungmin Kim
