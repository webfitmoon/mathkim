Opening Story: The Student Who Knows Everything but Runs Out of Time

Not long after Hyunwoo formed the habit of solving by hand, he ran straight into the next wall. When the paper arrived, he worked diligently from the first question onwards, one by one. Yet whenever the bell rang, the last three or four questions were still blank. When I marked the paper, some of those blanks turned out to be easy questions he could readily have solved.

This was not Hyunwoo’s problem alone. A student I taught some years ago, Minseo, was exactly the same. In fact, their grasp of the concepts was fairly solid. Ask them a question and they explained it fluently; their homework was meticulous. Yet every exam ended with the final few questions untouched. “Maybe I just don’t have a head for math. If I know the material, why do I keep running out of time?” they asked dejectedly. Rather than have them bring me the marked paper, I told Minseo to record the order in which they had solved the questions, together with the time spent on each. The next day’s record made the cause obvious at a glance. They had spent a full eleven minutes on Question 4. Had they simply pushed those eleven minutes to the end, they could have answered three more easy questions correctly. What Minseo lacked was neither ability nor a head for math.

“I don’t have enough time” is only half true. What Hyunwoo and Minseo lacked was not time but a method for using it. This chapter is not about methods of calculation. It is about time management: how to turn the same ability and the same fifty minutes into more marks.

The Real Reason You Run Out of Time: Management, Not Ability

The Trap of Solving in Order from the Front

Exam papers are generally arranged from easier questions at the front to harder ones at the back, but not always. One unusually awkward question is often planted in the middle. A student who solves only in order gets stuck on that one question and spends five minutes, then seven. Meanwhile, the questions farther back that they could have solved are washed away by the clock.

The Sunk-Cost Trap of Clinging to One Question

“I’ve come this far; it would be a waste to give up.” You have already spent three minutes, so it feels as though just a little longer will finish it. But that “little longer” consumes another three minutes. The time already spent will not return. You have to ask yourself, with a cool head: “Is this one minute best spent on this question, or on the two questions waiting behind it?”

I call what follows an “exam-room blackout.” You pour ten minutes into Question 14, suddenly look up at the clock, and discover that fifteen minutes remain. There are still five or six questions to solve.

At that instant your mind goes blank, cold sweat runs down your back, and even the easy questions that made sense moments ago begin to look like letters floating on the page. The sunk-cost trap is truly frightening because it steals time and then paralyses what remains with fear.

The Tragedy of Leaving Certain Marks Behind

The most painful marks are lost not because you answered incorrectly, but because you never saw the question. You could have solved it, yet ran out of time before you even touched it. That is a problem of management, not ability, which is precisely why it can be fixed.

I had a student like that too. In ordinary practice, this student worked at Grade 2 level, but every exam produced a Grade 4. When I inspected their papers, I found that they routinely spent nearly twenty minutes clinging to one difficult constructed-response question, then left four or five multiple-choice questions at the back completely blank. What they needed was not more study. They needed one change of order: take the questions you can solve first. As soon as they changed the order, they recovered Grade 2 in the very next exam. Their ability had not risen. The ability they already possessed had finally turned into marks.

When the Paper Arrives, Draw a Map First

Use the First Minute to Scan the Whole Paper

Do not charge at Question 1 as soon as the bell rings. Spend the first minute turning through the whole paper and reading the terrain. Where is it easy, where is it steep, and where are the high-mark questions? Two people can walk the same road at very different speeds depending on whether they know the map.

Take the Easy Questions and the Certain Marks First

Once you have scanned the paper, choose your order. Take the questions you can certainly solve and bank those marks quickly. This does two things. First, it prevents the tragedy of missing an easy question for lack of time. Second, banking marks early settles the mind, so you are less shaken when you reach a difficult question.

Budget Time by the Marks Available

If an exam is worth 100 marks and lasts fifty minutes, each mark is worth roughly thirty seconds. Spending five minutes on one four-mark question is the same as surrendering several three-mark questions you could have secured in that time. Marks are your time budget. There is no worse trade than clutching one difficult four-mark question and losing three easy questions worth three marks each.

Let us make the map more concrete. For a fifty-minute exam, I have students design a scenario in advance: “Set aside the two multiple-choice questions that look hardest and the final constructed-response question. Use the first thirty minutes for the initial scan and a first pass through everything else. Spend the next fifteen on the three questions set aside, and keep the final five for checking and marking.”

When the budget is allocated in advance, a difficult question cannot shake you: you know that a fifteen-minute reserve is still waiting. That is the difference between being chased by time and deploying it.

Three Rules for Commanding the Clock

Rule 1: The Two-Minute Rule — Mark It and Move On

If your hand has not found a way into a question after about two minutes, mark it with a star and move on. This is not retreat. It is regrouping. As you solve other questions, the clue to the one you left will often appear without warning. Returning after a break can help you look beyond the approach you first became fixed on.

Rule 2: The Two-Pass Strategy

On the first pass, solve only the questions you are sure of, quickly. Skip anything starred. Return to the stars on the second pass. You will then have secured every question you can solve, leaving all the remaining time to invest in the difficult ones. You are no longer being chased. You are in command.

Rule 3: Reserve the Final Five Minutes for Checking and Marking

If you cling to a new question until the bell, you may fail to transfer your answers or let mistakes slip through without a check. Keep the final five minutes empty, without exception, and use them to check the answer sheet and your calculations. This is when you use the “tracing back” habit from Chapter 2. Even after solving the whole paper, shifting every answer by one row while transferring them is more common than you might think.

One more point: these three rules cannot be switched on the morning of the exam. You must practise the two-minute rule and the two-pass strategy with a timer whenever you work through a problem book, so that your hand follows them automatically in the exam room. Time management is training too, and training belongs to the person who did it in advance.

Once Hyunwoo began using these three rules, the blank pages disappeared. His ability had not grown. He had begun to transfer all the ability he already possessed onto the exam paper.

Action Check

Three Things to Change Today from This Chapter

  1. Use the first minute after receiving the paper to scan the whole thing. Do not charge straight at Question 1.

  2. If you are stuck on one question for two minutes or more, star it and move on. Return on the second pass.

  3. Always reserve the final five minutes for checking and marking.

Parents’ Q&A

One Page for Parents

Q.
My child says, “I ran out of time and couldn’t finish.” Should I make them practise solving faster?
A.
Check the order before you worry about speed. Many students who run out of time are not slow with their hands; they lose the easy questions while clinging to one hard one. Ask, “Did you miss the easy ones because you were holding on to something difficult?”
Q.
My child handles problem books well in ordinary practice, but the exam marks are always low.
A.
The problem may be time management rather than ability. Even at home, have your child do “exam-condition practice,” solving against the actual exam time. Training to solve under pressure from the clock uses a completely different muscle from training to solve a problem.
Q.
How should we time practice at home?
A.
Set the timer for five minutes less than the real exam: forty-five minutes for a fifty-minute paper. Use the remaining five minutes to practise checking and marking the answer sheet (OMR). Adjust the plan to the school’s actual exam duration and answer format.
Q.
My child always comes home from an exam with the last part unfinished.
A.
The habit of solving strictly in order is the likely cause. They become trapped by a difficult question near the front and lose all the easy questions at the back. Have your child try it just once: “This time, put a star by the hard ones, move on, and come back later.” You will see the blank pages at the end shrink sharply.
Q.
My child is upset because there are so many questions they “got wrong through a silly mistake.”
A.
Many mistakes come not from a lack of ability but from having no time to check while being chased by the clock. Without the habit of reserving the final five minutes for checking, even questions your child knows will slip away. If mistakes are frequent, examine time management before increasing the volume of study.

In Closing: Time Is the Vessel That Carries Ability

However strong your ability, it cannot become marks unless you can fit it inside the fifty minutes called an exam. Time management is a rule, not a talent, and a rule can be changed today. As Hyunwoo discovered, blank pages are far more often the result of not knowing the right order than of lacking ability.

You now know how to solve by hand (Chapter 2) and command the clock (Chapter 3). One thing remains. However well you solve and manage the paper, not knowing what will appear still creates anxiety.
