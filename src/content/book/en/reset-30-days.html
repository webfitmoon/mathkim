If you do not know what to apply first from this book, or how, follow the thirty-day plan below exactly. Choose the one track that fits where you are now. Each track covers four weeks, roughly thirty days, with a goal and a set of actions for every week.

The grades here refer to the five-band system used for school grades under the 2028 framework. Choose your track less by the exact grade than by the place where you are stuck now.

Track A — Grades 1–2 (Top Band): “Eliminate Mistakes and Reach the Very Top”

Even strong students can have gaps in concepts. Identify the causes of lost marks first, then focus on solving familiar material reliably.

  • Week 1 — Map your mistakes: Sort questions missed on recent school exams and mock examinations into the four categories from Chapter 4: concept, calculation, condition, or time. This week’s goal is to discover which categories account for your lost marks.

  • Week 2 — Retrace the questions you got right: Following Chapter 4, find faster and more secure routes through questions you answered correctly. Train not for the answer, but for speed and stability.

  • Week 3 — Reverse-engineer three years of past papers: Extract the tools that recur across three years of your school’s past papers, like the relationship between roots and coefficients in Chapters 2 and 4. Practise using the same tool under different conditions.

  • Week 4 — Optimise time + constructed responses: Cement the time-management method from Chapter 3 through forty-five minutes of solving and five of checking and marking, and check the causes of lost marks in constructed-response questions one by one.

Track B — Around Grade 3 (Middle Band): “Turn Concepts into Weapons”

You seem to know the material, but cannot produce it in the exam. Bring each concept to a state in which you can use it.

  • Week 1 — Weave a concept net: Following Chapter 5, draw a concept map for every unit you have studied. Connect functions, equations, and inequalities on one page.

  • Week 2 — Establish the Five Stages of Decoding: Use the Five Stages of Decoding from Chapter 2 on five questions a day. Replace the habit of solving by eye with solving by hand.

  • Week 3 — Build signals + classify wrong answers: Organise the “condition → concept” signals from Chapter 5 and sort missed questions into the four categories from Chapter 4.

  • Week 4 — Focus on one weak unit + practise for forty-five minutes: Choose your weakest unit and secure it again from the concepts up. At the weekend, practise forty-five minutes of solving followed by five of checking and marking.

Track C — Grades 4–5 (Starting from the Foundations): “Back to the Roots, but Quickly”

What you need now is not ambition, but the right first step. Begin with exactly three habits.

  • Week 1 — Hand, not eyes: Following Chapter 2, do only three questions a day, but write each one out completely by hand. Cover the worked solution and reach the point where you can solve it again unaided.

  • Week 2 — Retrieve root concepts on a blank page: Write the central concepts of Common Mathematics — equations and functions — on a blank page in your own words. This is the retrieval training from Chapter 5.

  • Week 3 — Circle the conditions: Build the Chapter 1 habit of marking the conditions in a question. Start slowly with easier past-paper questions.

  • Week 4 — Become conscious of time + one line on each error: Develop the sense of time from Chapter 3, then organise each missed question with the two-line error notebook from Chapter 4.

After Thirty Days

The aim of this thirty-day plan is not to promise a higher grade, but to review how you study and build a habit. Once the habit has settled, the mark has room to move. Do not rush. As Hyunwoo discovered, change begins at the point where the method changes.

After Four Weeks: Five Questions to Ask Yourself

After a month, being able to answer the five questions below matters more than the report card. Parents can check them with their child; students can check them alone.

Action Check

Thirty-Day Check

  1. Hand: Does the margin of my latest exam paper contain “given” and “required” in my own handwriting?

  2. Signals: For a question I got wrong, can I tell whether I missed the “one-line condition” or failed to call up the tool?

  3. Wrong answers: Is my error notebook two lines of “cause + prescription,” rather than copied questions?

  4. Time: Before an exam, do I plan forty-five minutes for solving and five for checking, or adjust that allocation to my own exam?

  5. Process: Even if the mark is unchanged, is there at least one problem where I am one step less stuck than I was yesterday?
